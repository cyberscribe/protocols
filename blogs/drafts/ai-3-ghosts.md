# To embrace our AI future, we must exorcise three ghosts from our collective past

"Now that this new technology can help us do more, better, and faster, let's use it to get rid of people so that we can keep doing what we have always done with less" -- said no team that believed in its mission, ever. [1]

Yet everywhere I see contraction in the face of AI's expansive possibilities -- not only in slashing headcounts, but even more dangerously, in collapsing our own creative thinking about the future. Fearing change seems inherent to the human condition. But this change, and this fear, feels different.

The inquisitors who burned William Tyndale at the stake in 1536 for printing the Bible in English could not see beyond Latin Scripture into Reformation Europe. The tailors who broke into Barthélemy Thimonnier's factory to destroy his newly-invented sewing machines could not see beyond their stitchwork into twentieth-century fashion. Despite those rich futures, noone can see a place for themselves in a world they cannot imagine.

Yet AI anxiety goes deeper than a lack of imagination alone.

I believe the grip of AI anxiety we feel is actually our own throats tightening unconsciously. AI, by its nature, has resurrected three ghosts from our collective past. "I never thought of it that way, but it makes sense" has been a common reaction when I introduce my friends to these ghosts, and tell their tale. Sometimes, too, a sigh of relief.

If you dare to keep reading, you too will be visited by three ghosts. My hope is that in meeting each one, you may find that you recognise them. In recognising them, I hope that they will haunt you less.

The first ghost, **Negative Anthropomorphic Projection**, appears as a pumpkin-headed scarecrow with flickering eyes and a rictus grin. Our fear of its appearance is vestigial -- once evolutionarily useful, now misplaced. In reality, all he wants is to sing a song about his longing to have a brain.

The second ghost, **Collective Subjugation Anxiety**, is a two-headed giant, stitched together from our shared ancestral heritage of having been both "conqueror" and "conquered" within the same family tree.

The third ghost is the most chilling of all, a spectral **black swan** whose presence commands a deafening silence. Reanimated for our generation by the Covid-19 pandemic, her icy quietude leads to amnesia, paranoia, and despair.

Despite these theatrical disembodiments of our collective anxieties, I have no intention of reducing AI itself to a comical bulge in a funhouse mirror. AI resembles human thinking spookily well at times, and speeds us up by co-thinking.

AI is powerful, fast, and, like a rickety rollercoaster at a cheap fun fair, it thrills us partly because it feels dangerous. 

When will it go off the rails?

If Negative Anthropomorphic Projection, Collective Subjugation Anxiety, and The Black Swan of Silence have their way, it's any moment now. Perpetually. Always right around the next bend.

So hold on to your hats, and let's get to ghost busting.

The recurrent and sometimes lengthy stay-at-home orders during the pandemic went against some fundamental presumptions of capitalism. For knowledge workers, it challenged the in-office, five-a-week, nine-to-five minimum with school-as-childcare norm. For those who could not work from home, unprecedented government spending (in some cases [more than 4% of GDP](https://unctad.org/news/unprecedented-covid-19-stimulus-packages-are-not-being-leveraged-accelerate-sdg-investment)) worldwide struck at a deeper tenet: if you don't work to earn your bread, you deserve to starve, because there is (still) not enough to go around. These assumptions may be challenged again in the AI era.

I is not the first thing that has demanded we reimagine ourselves, but unlike the pandemic it is not going away.
