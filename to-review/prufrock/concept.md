**The Prufrock Protocol**

Poetry is among the oldest long nows we have. For four thousand years, it has bound the long — timeless themes — to the now — concrete individual moments — in a single act. Poetry is a human timepiece that predates the clock.

In a world forever changed by generative AI, where words and images can be synthesised quickly and at scale, the urgency of the form is renewed. Sampling and recording subjective experience longitudinally in an authenticated form.

The protocol measures time by randomly interrupting a moment in an individual's life, confronting them with a response to someone else's prior moment of interruption, and recording their reply. That reply forms the basis for the next interruption, somewhere else, of someone else. The record preserves time and location and is cryptographically signed. Each chain accretes into a poem — at once unique to its moments and continuous with the timeless themes poetry has always engaged.

Most cryptographic ledgers attempt to solve issues of financial trust. Prufrock explores their use in authenticating situated human experience instead — a need now more urgent than the one they were built for. The collapse of centralised banking remains theoretical; the collapse of our trust in words and images has arrived.

Poetry is also our oldest compression algorithm. Where generative AI averages human concerns toward the statistical mean, poetry distils them into specific instances of individual consciousness in time and space — giving specificity to the timeless, in what Marvin Bell called the infinite game of using words to transcend words. AI fills the negative space of meaning with quantifiable certainty; poetry preserves it for the reader. Prufrock situates itself as antidote to the hegemony of anti-poetic AI.

A prototype would use the smartphone for interruption, confrontation, and reply. An initial trial could engage fourteen poets composing fourteen-line poems over fourteen days, each interrupted once daily at a random time within their local waking hours. Subsequent rounds would extend beyond literary practice to protocol theorists and other communities new to poetry.

In an era of synthetic media and accelerated attention, the question is whether the oldest long-now form might mark a new kind of time — small, signed moments of human expression, brought together across the world and across the ages. Poetry always has. The protocol asks whether we can do it together, in real time, at scale, as continual proof someone, somewhere, was alive.
