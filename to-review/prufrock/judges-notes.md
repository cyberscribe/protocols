# Selection Notes — The Prufrock Protocol

*Long Now Foundation × Protocols Institute, 02026 Portfolio*
*Reviewer notes — internal*

---

I came to this submission expecting a poetics dressed up in protocol vocabulary. What I found was the rarer thing: a protocol that takes poetry seriously as evidence, and that, almost incidentally, restores Prufrock to us as a working figure rather than a literary punchline.

The central move is to treat hesitation — Eliot's *"Do I dare?"* — not as a failure of nerve but as a civilizational instrument. In an attention economy that has industrialized the conversion of consideration into commitment, the deliberate refusal to act on the first available impulse is no longer cowardice; it is infrastructure. The Prufrock Protocol formalizes that refusal into something practiceable, transmissible, and — importantly for the Institute's interests — compositional with other protocols rather than antagonistic to them.

## Why it clears the long-now bar

Most submissions in this cycle interpreted the 10,000-year horizon as a scale problem: bigger clocks, deeper archives, slower media. Peake reads it as a cadence problem. The protocol's unit of work is the daily act of noticing — `notice(inner, outer)` — accumulated across a life into what he calls, with admirable lack of ceremony, doing one's life's work in forty-five minutes a day. This is the rare long-now proposal whose method one could begin practising tomorrow morning and whose measurement horizon still extends past one's grandchildren. The temporal compression is exactly inverted from the usual mistake.

## Why it clears the Protocols bar

The submission distinguishes cleanly between first-order rules (the grammar of a form — sonnet, ghazal, haiku) and second-order rules (the meta-protocol of *learn, break, remake, break again*), then invokes Gödelian incompleteness to argue that the second-order layer is not aesthetic flourish but a structural necessity for any system that wants to handle truths it cannot internally prove. This is a stronger argument for protocol layering than most of the network-stack analogies the Institute usually fields. It also generalises: the same recursion shows up in legal systems, scientific revision, and mature engineering practice. Poetry is the case study, not the constraint.

The Tay Bridge counter-example earns its keep. McGonagall as the canonical demonstration of what happens when a practitioner has neither learned the first-order rules nor earned the right to break them — a cleaner illustration of protocol violation than a thousand TCP diagrams.

## Trade-offs, named honestly

**Authentic.** Very strong. The author is a working poet with decades of accumulated practice and the document carries the unmistakable density of having been lived in. Nothing here was composed by extrapolation.

**Principled.** Strong. The treatment of the fool — Kyoami in Kurosawa's *Ran*, the benshi, the licensed truth-teller standing outside the protocols of the court — gives the project an ethical centre that resists optimization-talk. The fool can speak truth precisely because they are unprofitable. Long Now needs more of this.

**Effective.** This is where I have the most concern, and I want to name it rather than dress it up. The Prufrock Protocol's adoption surface is narrower than its merits warrant. It asks the practitioner to slow down inside a culture that treats slowing down as weakness, and the proposal does not yet have a strong answer for distribution. I would like to see, in the next round, a clearer account of how the protocol propagates without diluting — how the daily-poet practice survives contact with people who do not already love poetry. The Institute may have intuitions here.

## Recommendation

Accept into the portfolio. Pair with funding for a transmission study: how does this practice land in domains that do not self-identify as literary? My instinct is that the protocol will turn out to be most useful exactly where it is least expected — among engineers, clinicians, and operators — and the worst outcome would be for it to be archived as a poetry project.

The protocol's own epigraph is the right one to close on:

> No permanence is ours; we are the flood that flows to fit whatever form it finds.

Long Now has been waiting for a submission that understands this is a practice, not a slogan.
