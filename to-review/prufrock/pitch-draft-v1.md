# The Prufrock Protocol

*A pitch to The Long Now Foundation, in conversation with the Protocols Institute*

**Robert Peake — May 2026 — draft v1**

---

## Thesis

Poetry is the oldest engineered protocol for transmitting compressed cultural state across long time horizons. Its fundamental algorithm — *learn the rules, break the rules, make new rules, break the new rules* — is not what corrupts the transmission; it is what makes the transmission durable.

## Why "Prufrock"

Prufrock could not commit. The protocol bears his name because hesitation — *compromise plus conflict* — is the engine of every poem worth keeping. It is also the central tension at the heart of every long-running protocol: between specification and adaptation, between fidelity and survival. Prufrock's question — *"Do I dare?"* — is the question every protocol designer asks before a breaking change.

## Why now

Three reasons converge.

First, large language models have made the *first-order* protocols of language — grammar, meter, even genre — trivially recombinable. What they cannot yet do, on the evidence, is the *second-order* move: break the rules in ways that surprise and signify. This makes poetry's algorithm legible as engineering for the first time outside a literature classroom — and visible as something worth preserving deliberately.

Second, the Protocols Institute and adjacent communities have begun to treat protocols as a category of cultural infrastructure deserving their own discipline. Poetry has been left out of that taxonomy. It shouldn't be.

Third, the durability question is acute. Most contemporary poetry circulates inside platforms whose half-lives are short. A monograph that codifies the protocol layer of poetry is the kind of artefact that survives the platform.

## Lineage

This work sits in conversation with three Long Now precedents.

**Brian Eno's generative work** treats rule-systems as the medium itself. Prufrock Protocol extends that move from sound to language.

**The Rosetta Disk** preserves languages as parallel texts. This project complements it by codifying the *protocols by which* poems compress cultural state inside those languages.

**Long Bets** formalizes prediction as a speech act with rules and consequences. Prufrock Protocol asks whether other speech acts — elegy, vow, witness — can be similarly specified.

## The protocol stack — one paragraph

A poem is a layered protocol. *First-order rules* are the linguistic substrate: phonology, grammar, the strict ordering of English adjectives, Welsh consonant mutations. *Second-order rules* are formal: meter, stanza, ghazal radif, sonnet volta. The fundamental algorithm is recursive — P(r) = B(L(r)) → r′. Learn r, break it via L and B, produce r′, then iterate. Breaking without learning produces McGonagall's *Tay Bridge Disaster* — a poem about a bridge collapse that is itself an aesthetic collapse. Learning without breaking produces fluent LLM output that scores well on rubric and dies on the page. The protocol is the recursion.

## v1 deliverable

A short printed monograph — *Protocols of Poetry* — that codifies the framework with worked examples. Roughly 80–120 pages, designed to sit beside the Rosetta Disk on a Long Now reference shelf rather than on a trade table. Contents:

- The taxonomy (P_M / P_NC / P_S)
- The first- / second-order distinction
- The recursive algorithm with worked derivation
- Ten case studies drawn primarily from public-domain sources (Whitman, Eliot's *Prufrock*, McGonagall, classical ghazal in translation, Welsh cynghanedd, Brecht in fair-use excerpt)
- An LLM stress-test appendix demonstrating where current models satisfy first-order rules but fail the second-order break

Paired with a Long Now seminar where the framework is delivered live and the audience writes one stanza inside it.

## v2 — deferred, named for transparency

An open, versioned corpus of "protocol-poems" — public-domain texts annotated with the rules they enact, the rules they break, and the residue r′. Released under a permissive licence so the corpus can outlive the project. Not part of this ask; named so the trajectory is legible.
