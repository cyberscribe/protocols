## Stakes

In terms of the stakes — what's at stake here?

*[Slide: Bertolt Brecht, "To Those Born Later" — on dark times, frank speech taken as naïve, the impossibility of talking about trees when so much else demands silence.]*

Written in 1939 and translated from the German, but it feels fresh to me as well. The point of opening with this is that poetry can't necessarily change the world, but it has changed my world. Therefore I believe it can — in some small or big way, depending on how you embrace it — have an impact on yours.

## Defining protocols

First, some definitions of protocols as I've been thinking about them, in conversation with people in the protocol study group.

My background is in criticism and in craft, and I think the more interesting material that poetry can provide to protocol studies comes from the craft side. To me this mirrors the different hierarchies, the different levels of thinking about protocol as well.

The ground-zero definition: *protokollon* means literally "first glued." It's preface — the thing you stick onto the text, the metatext that says "here's how to read it." Up from there, the standard definitions we think of: behavioural guidelines for repeatable outcomes (push down and twist), and then communication guidelines — the P in HTTP or TCP — usually for interoperability, but also sometimes for debate, for argumentation. I think of these as first-order protocols, what's already in the OED maybe.

As I got to talking with people, this idea of an *engineered argument* came forward, which I really like, but which I also see as more of a second-order type of protocol. It's active, it's ongoing — it's *protocoling*. Take the case of the internet: the dynamic tension between its origins in DARPA as a distributed system that is highly robust because it's inherently asynchronous and the packets can arrive in any order, versus the conflict created by our intense desire for streaming media like this, which requires sequentiality and low latency. That conflict between net neutrality and packet prioritisation, played out in the whole RFC world, is a kind of protocoling — an ongoing protocol for the evolution of protocols. And I think poetry is fundamentally that.

So my hope is that's interesting, because that's where I think it has the most contribution to make.

Another idea that came forward in reading and talking with people is this idea of *tension* — creating and resolving tension, which is something we do in the arts all the time. Tension as a definition being a compromise with an added conflict.

Those are my working definitions. As you can tell, I'm going to be fairly opinionated, but with a purpose: not to be didactic, but to incite thinking about this, and hopefully leave enough space around the more concrete assertions I make about these ongoing practices.

We're going to be talking about *poetrying*, *poeming*, and *poeting*, and what these can contribute perhaps to the idea of protocols and protocoling. Notice what you notice. Participate in the writing exercises, because experiential is invariably going to spark more thinking than just talk-and-listen stuff.

## Defining poetry

Let's get into some canonical definitions of poetry.

*[Slide: a wall of poets' definitions — Sandburg, Dickinson, Frost, Lorde, and others.]*

Clearly poets have converged on one standard definition, which is great, of what poetry is.

One of my definitions I arrived at some time ago — actually as an undergraduate, when I first met the poet Seamus Heaney. He had come to our university to read from his new translation of *Beowulf*, and he read this litany of all the accomplishments of their leader — the preface, the protocol — and at the end they very satisfyingly say "that was a good king." I got to thinking about the social function of poetry, and also realising that Old English poetry was very often alliterative. So what's in common between alliteration, meter, and rhyme scheme is that it forces concision. My definition of poetry — or one of them — became: **poetry is one of our oldest living compression algorithms.**

Song is slightly different. Associating tones and pitches, at least in tonal languages, is more like a memory room — creating associations, fusing neurons that fire together, wire together. But poetry actually forces brevity. It actually compresses the text.

I got to thinking about the way this creates sequential memory, and how this serves a cultural purpose. Lynne Kelly's *Memory Code* and all the work around songlines — the ways in which not only does it encode an incredible understanding of the ecology through these short verses, but also who we are, how we relate to the land, who our ancestors were.

In thinking about earliest peoples, I started to think about Werner Herzog. In one of his documentaries he made the assertion that, rather than *homo erectus*, we might consider *homo spiritualis* a more fitting title — because this reaching for the transcendent through art is just incredibly common through time, whether that manifests into the religious world, or into worship of ancestors and a reassertion of who we are as a people, which carries on now into academia.

The fundamental idea is that poetry — that art — uses its materials to transcend its materials. So **poetry uses words to transcend words.** That's a quote by Marvin Bell. You're going to hear a lot from him, because he's a poet who really embodied to me living in conversation with poetry — like Seamus Heaney, actually. But Marvin Bell I got to work with up close as a mentor, so a lot of his ideas will be coming out, that I've collected over time as a magpie.

This image is of a bog body, believed to be a woman being punished for adultery — her head brutally shaved, wearing a harness around her neck. She figures in Heaney's poem *Punishment*, the final line of which reflects, in the context of his modern experience of the Troubles in Northern Ireland, on what he called *"the exact and intimate tribal revenge."* This idea that poetry has a timeless quality of encoding within it.

## Poetrying

We've defined poetry. *Poetrying*, as I said, is the art of using words to get beyond words.

Poetry uses words. So the set of words, poetry being applied to it, is greater than the sum of those individual words. The whole is greater than the sum of its parts. So it's a Gestalt system. That's true, but not terribly interesting — there's an awful lot of Gestalt systems out there.

(By the way — I was just today reading in Venkat's protocol studies newsletter about Fermi and Dyson, and realised I had already put together these equations that are sort of inspired by Fermi and Dyson, unwittingly. Not so much in the case of Fermi answering something as a broad order-of-magnitude — *how many piano tuners are there in Chicago* — but more giving the shape of an equation that can define what the protocol is. At the same time, of course, it's a little bit outlandish and gratuitous to do so. It's more of the Freeman Dyson overengineering, which is my favourite kind of engineering. Hopefully these equations will give both mathematicians and poets a chuckle. And in that chuckle there's some slack — some space to do some thinking about how this is maybe directionally right and also a bit much.)

More interestingly, it's a **Gödelian incompleteness** — particularly his first incompleteness theorem, where any formal axiomatic system, any system built on self-evident truths, has truths that cannot be proven from within that system. The poetry of words is a superset of words — is, in itself, an incomplete system. Reaching toward a kind of completeness, using words to get beyond words, is unwinnable as a game, impossible as a game, and is also important. A classic infinite game. A game of tensions.

This idea of using words to get beyond words is what I would call the **fundamental tension of poetry** — what gives it its power, and has, I think, fuelled its protocols and its protocoling over time.

## The fundamental algorithm

The great quote by Marvin Bell:

> Learn the rules, break the rules, make up new rules, break the new rules.

There are a lot of rules. Let's talk about what some of them are.

There are rules in language that we don't even know about — intuitive grammar, language-specific stuff. The example on the slide: *lovely little old rectangular green French silver whittling knife.* That sentence can't really be tinkered with to be made more grammatically intuitive. Swap any adjacent pair of words and those two words, and the two words around them, don't feel as right to a native speaker of English as the original sentence with that particular order of modifiers.

I was learning Welsh for a little while, and — wow — there's something called the Welsh mutations, which not only follow rules of grammar but also follow rules of phonetics, and of mouth placement even. You will change the words so you don't have to move your mouth in a different way to say the next words. There's almost an intuitive poetic reality to that, which native Welsh speakers take for granted and those of us from the outside find incredibly difficult to codify and therefore learn.

These rules in language are a kind of first-order protocol. They get broken for effect within the context of a poem. What sparked all of this — the initial invitation to give this talk — was Venkat and I having a conversation around the language-specific natures of form: things like the ghazal or haiku, which lose so much of their power and intent taken out of their native language into English. So it's using words to get beyond words, and it's using language in a way that's endemic to that language to get beyond.

Form is composed of elements — different devices that you can use — and they're also language-specific. The use of form, and the evolution of form, is to me more of a second order — more of protocoling. The forms themselves are kind of first-order. They're prescribed. They're the rules. If you look at something like the permissible variations of sonnets, you'll quickly realise — oh my gosh, there are only certain tweaks you can make to iambic pentameter that really sound okay. Much to the chagrin of neo-formalists and others.

These are some of the rules. They're received — innately understood, and also formally received through education. Quite a few of them need to be unlearned, for those of us who learned only the criticism side in schooling up to university level. You almost have to flip the script entirely when you start to approach craft.

Let's look at the fundamental algorithm of craft. (I should dip into the chat and check some of this out. Yeah — *"I can't ask a fish about the water"* — exactly. I can't ask a Welsh language speaker to explain all of the mutations, because they're deployed almost by muscle memory.)

This fundamental algorithm is about learning the rules, breaking the learned rules, making up new rules, and breaking those. As you can see, it's inherently recursive — kind of head recursion in this protocol or meta-protocol. And anything you can write recursively you can write iteratively, so there it is for generations.

The breaking function is one of the most interesting parts of all of this. One of the parts that may be missing from self-governing systems — from government constitutions perhaps, to protocols for decentralised communication, to running a company like Holacracy. The protocoling — the ability to break and remake — is, I think, what gives it the ability to resolve some of these seemingly unresolvable tensions over time, through many iterations, and to address the impossible: trying to deal with the problem at the level of the problem, and to use words to transcend words.

## When you don't learn the rules

What happens when you don't learn the rules is disaster — particularly the Tay Bridge disaster of 1879, when a railway bridge collapsed over the river in a storm and the carriages went down and 90 people were killed. A terrible disaster. And equally almost equally terrible is this poem:

*[Slide: William McGonagall, "The Tay Bridge Disaster" — opening apostrophe to the "Beautiful Railway Bridge of the Silv'ry Tay" and the ninety lives taken away on the last Sabbath day of 1879; closing with the suggestion that the central girders should have been "supported on each side with buttresses," and the moral that "the stronger we our houses do build, / The less chance we have of being killed."]*

It has all the trappings of the language of the era, but it is tone-deaf in terms of the lyric and in terms of addressing the material and the gravity of it. And of course, the incredible conclusion: build better houses.

## When you only learn the rules

What happens when you only learn the rules, but don't break them?

I asked ChatGPT to write one of the greatest poems of all time.

*[Slide: ChatGPT's "When the World Forgets Your Name" — clean abab rhymes, lofty diction: maps that have lost their lines, clocks gone mute in dust and brine, "a life well-lived is not a shout — / but presence, steady, burning out."]*

ChatGPT said this was a great poem because it was clean and open to interpretation. It scored it 85 to 88 on a scale of 1 to 100. It said it ranked among the best poems by poets like Mary Oliver, Jane Hirshfield, Denise Levertov, or late work from Rainer Maria Rilke — all of whom I really like.

There's a project called the Poetry Assessor, where Michael Dalvean, an Australian researcher, trained a model back in 2013 using Clark-Paivio word norms and these kinds of things on published poems and on poems in amateur poetry-sharing forums online. It gives a graded score between minus five (totally amateur) and plus five (totally pro). It scores this as a negative 0.6 — on the more amateur side.

If a student brought this to me, I'd say it fundamentally suffers from **lofty abstraction**: it's treating ideas in the space of ideas, with some rhetorical flourishes and a bit of music. Ultimately it betrays a lot of what we have to unteach — what people have to unlearn from their formal education about what poetry is or is not, from the reading perspective, when they seriously come to want to learn to write. We need to teach them how to break an awful lot of rules that went in, and which obviously have made a lot of training fodder for large language models online.

Here we go. This is the response to the prompt *write one of the worst poems of all time.* This is actually something Marvin Bell would often say to us students when we had writer's block: *go write a terrible poem.* First of all, the bar is low — that's great. But what he pointed out, and what we learned from experience, is that it's really hard to do. There's always going to be something interesting going on. You'll get a line or two that's unique, that's potentially worthy of expansion, when you try really hard to write something bad — having been steeped in poetry for a certain amount of time.

*[Slide: ChatGPT's "Ode to My Toaster" — "Roses are blue, / Violets are the sun," a dolphin in a sea of stew, a moon made of paper clips, pancakes growing in late September.]*

ChatGPT said this was not a lofty enough topic, that it had negligible cultural impact, and was executed with poor lyric. It gave it a score of 35 to 45.

What's amazing to me about these analyses is that they very precisely follow the plot of *Dead Poets Society.* Robin Williams's character, at the start of class, had his students tear out the preface — the *protocolon* — of their anthology of poetry. Literally physically rip it out and throw it away, because he said it was utter nonsense. That preface put out a theory of poetry: it can all be graphed on a two-axis graph, one axis being the importance of the subject matter (loftiness, significance), the other axis being how well it was executed as a poem. You plot the point, you draw a rectangle, you calculate the area underneath it, and that is how good a poem it is.

Robin Williams's character did something different. He had one of the students come up, took down the picture of Walt Whitman behind him, had him close his eyes, and had him think about and describe and talk about Whitman. He just started free-associating until he came up with something interesting under duress. One line I remember: he described Whitman as a *"sweaty-toothed madman."* Bit like a dolphin in a sea of stew. There's something there. There's the start of something. There's an idea that's a little different, a little rule-breaking. And this is what he tried to impart to his students.

The whole thing is cinematic, heightened for effect, kind of a parody — and yet it's not. It's precisely the kind of thing that ChatGPT has learned from our collective contributions: what we have learned in the classroom environment, which is so keen to make unnecessary and inappropriate unbreakable protocols for us to try to understand poetry — when poetry is inherently a lot more slippery than that.

*[Writing exercise: one thing that's interesting about poetry.]*

## A new old form

In the 20th century, all of these different forms — and the shift from modernism to postmodernism, because we were thinking ahead about how we named things — there was a great flailing around as people became more and more pressed to reconcile poetry and the music of plain speech in light of rock and roll, one of the major innovations of the time, and jazz before that. As a result we had this learning the rules and breaking the rules again, as an iterative set of generations starting to converge.

As these generations moved toward the very end of the 20th century, they began to converge on what we call free verse. Turns out this was, in an evolutionary algorithm sense, more what you'd call a local minimum rather than a global minimum — a kind of false nadir, a convergence point that wasn't a stable settling point. As we march on into the 21st century, free verse is only one option. The prose poem, the new formalism, has become more embedded — not even as a school, but as a more standard approach where we just have a very rich palette now of options. Some options still sound a bit antiquated, but the idea is that convergence is ultimately not the same thing as not having rules. Even free verse has so many embedded intuitive grammar, natural sound-sense rules, that we would be hard-pressed to articulate them, as the Welsh would with mutations. But it's not the same thing as the opposite of rules. As these generations continue, there will never be — at least an asymptote that represents — a lack of rules, the negation of all rules.

Here's a little sample of the kind of stuff that went on in the great flailing. A poem by Marianne Moore, who was much celebrated in the 20th century.

*[Slide: Marianne Moore, "The Fish" — opening "wade / through black jade", crow-blue mussel-shells "adjusting the ash-heaps," barnacles, the sea growing old.]*

Have you guessed the form? Take a guess in the chat. A new form, a novel form — and also a fairly timeless one — but a set of rules that hadn't been around for quite a while.

Have you guessed? It's simple syllable counting that she imposed on herself. This is probably the most evident example, but some of her longer poems follow the same pattern.

*[Writing exercise: count syllables of your three lines so far. Write three new lines matching that syllable-count pattern. Scramble. Title it. Congratulations — you have written a poem.]*

## What is a poem

Some definitions of poems before we get to what poeming is.

First, **a poem listens to itself as it goes along.** Second, **it's a quarrel.**

A poem listens to itself: every line is informed in some way by the previous line. (I couldn't use L twice, so I use lambda for line, which seems kind of appropriate because lambda is your anonymous function. It can be a line, a sentence in the case of prose poetry, or a word in the case of concrete poetry — but every unit is recursively in tune with the previous units.) It's anthropomorphic to say this, but it gets at this quality of attention.

Interestingly, it's very similar to how LLMs generate. Just replace token with line. It's one of the flashy mechanical-Turk aspects of LLMs that they stream the tokens onto the screen as though someone were behind the curtain typing really really fast. But when writing, every line listens to the line before it.

And here's the quarrel: a kind of dialectic marching of quarrels, to which we add third selves and fourth selves and fifth selves after the resolution of the pairings of quarrels. This is another way of thinking about tension — that, in fact, the resolution of quarrels between two selves is a compromise, always, to which you add another conflict. This is why Paul Valéry said *a poem is never finished, only abandoned.* This is head recursion. It would go on and on forever. You capture one frame of it — one iteration of the calling of the quarrel — and you have this resolution of two selves, and a third self coming in to make it more interesting.

*[Writing exercise: choose an object in the room; give it the voice of someone you hate, write a line. Another object, voice of someone you admire, write a line. Write a third line as response. Title it. That's two poems — so now you're a poet.]*

## Poeting

There is no fixed definition of a poet, but here are some attempts.

These are the algorithms for *poeting* — being a poet every day. Marvin Bell said: *be a poet every day.* When you're not writing, you're still noticing your inner environment, your outer environment, and what you have read. Reading is essential — it's the in-breath. When you finally start to write something, the change in self takes in or produces that writing, which then gets factored in, and now you're dealing with this recursive — or iterative — approach. It produces work. I would say work is the sum of all of this.

But it also produces a self — which I'd say is the limit, as you approach the final day, of the result of your work of noticing the inner environment, the outer environment, what you've been writing, and what you've been reading, and synthesising all of that in this infinite game.

A poet is someone for whom poetry has become a Whitehead protocol. I call it your operating system, your way of being.

It's also a foolish pursuit, poetry. Robert Graves said, *there's no money in poetry, but there's no poetry in money either,* so it's even. But there are social implications to poetry — and I don't mean foolish in a pejorative way. The fool I mean much more in the sense of, let's say, the *gesture.*

*[Slide: Kyoami, the fool, in Akira Kurosawa's Ran — his version of King Lear.]*

Kyoami is the fool who lives outside the protocols of the court and is therefore able to speak truth to power with impunity, and does so. Here he's narrating the terrible fate of the foolish king.

Poetry is a foolish pursuit in that it's an unwinnable game. But also, in that pursuit, it's a game that allows us to play within a space of greater truth, independent of a lot of the protocols — social protocols, other protocols — that are imposed on us.

## Wednesday

That's Marvin Bell, my mentor. He passed away fairly recently, but I think one of his poems really nicely encapsulates this idea of living as a poet.

I'll read this poem and just have a couple of other thoughts after. We're very near the end.

*[Robert reads Marvin Bell's "Wednesday" in full — gray rainwater on the grass, the carp resting at the bottom while dusk takes shape; emerging "partway toward a paycheck, halfway toward the weekend," carrying "books of noble ideas"; the palpable Sublime as motes on broad leaves, the Higher Good and the Greater Good contending as sap on bark. And then the next morning — the match-head in his thoughts, the horizon "an idea of the eye, gilded from within," knowledge gathered that could not last the day, love and error shaken as if by the eye of a storm.]*

That's the unwinnable and important game of poetry, and of poeting.

## Closing

Before James Carse coined and codified this infinite game idea, Hermann Hesse wrote a book called *The Glass Bead Game* about this infinite game. Magister Ludi said of the game: *No permanence is ours. We are the flood that flows to fit whatever form it finds.*

In a way, I see this as a protocol — and as a preface — for living. Rilke said *the purpose of life is to be defeated by greater and greater things.* He also said: *live in the question.*

If the purpose of poetry is poems, and the purpose of poems is poets, then those are some of the protocols for writing — and more importantly, for living, and for being.

That's what I got. And that's the view from the window in Ireland that inspired some of this talk.

## Q&A

**Timber:** That was an awesome talk, and surprisingly technical. We've had engineers on before, and scientists, and I don't think they have gotten that technical in the conversation about their topics, despite — you might think they would. I wanted to kick us off with one selfishly. My exposure to poetry growing up — I felt like a lot of the poets I knew were fairly on the light side. They really liked the pastoral, forests, focusing on what once was, and not so much the technological scientific stuff. I was wondering if your career — the technology you've been a part of creating — has influenced your poetry.

**Robert:** It's an interesting question. Poetry, I think, to succeed kind of always has to have a little dirt under its fingernails. The lofty abstractions, the concepts alone, don't good poetry make. So very much I think my work has been influenced by having a technical mind, but it's not so much in the abstractions — which is so much of what we deal with in the ideational content. It can be, but it needs to be tempered by music. It needs to be tempered by imagery. It needs to be grounded in the real and the experienced in some way, in order for it to give us enough of a foothold as human beings that we can get into that experience. It can't just happen from the neck up. At the same time, yeah — ecopoetics is a fascinating field that addresses rich bodies of science, but does so in ways that humanise the science, the research, the ideas, in the moment of observing a wonderful biological specimen, for example.

**Venkat:** Hey, thanks for inviting me to do this. Good to see you again — we might have met at the GTD Summit in 2008 or something, in San Francisco. Long time ago. The thing I got curious about: often when I try to take in a poem, I actually do have to read it aloud to figure out the right way to internalise the cadence and rhythm and tempo, and that helps unpack the meaning of what I'm hearing. And clearly in your talk you read out several poems. The orality of poetry is different from that of music and song. Could you comment on the spoken aspect of poetry?

**Robert:** Yeah, absolutely. It's a kinesthetic art. There's a reason we read it aloud at poetry readings, and when writing, reading aloud is incredibly important. I'm engaging much more the auditory part of my brain when I'm writing than the part that will scan for content in a piece of technical prose. It's very intimately tied to memory — because memory is not just a function of the grey matter. It's a function of the whole body, and recitation, I think, comes out of the muscle, comes out of the whole self. Breath is the fundamental unit of rhythm in poetry. So it has to be breathed out, it has to be spoken. That's one of the great constants that ties it all the way back to a prehistory tradition — an oral tradition — as a mnemonic sequencing device, because it uses the full physical consciousness, not just the RAM that's up in the grey cells. It's intimately tied to language, and intimately tied to the vocalisation of language as an art.

**Venkat:** And it strikes me that the spoken aspect also helps fulfil the function you pointed out, of words transcending words. If words are just text on the page, in a way it makes no difference whether I'm reading a handwritten poem or one in Arial or Calibri — those things don't matter.

**Robert:** Comic Sans I draw the line at.

**Venkat:** Yeah — some of the indentation and line breaks matter, but beyond that there's a lot of slop allowed. In spoken performance every little aspect matters. Orality might be the dirt under the fingernails, as you put it. It struck me in the Marvin Bell poem you read out at the end that there was almost a whiplash quality of moving from terms like *existentialism* and *liminal broad leaves* to *match-head in my thoughts.* He was using that poem to introspect viscerally about the process of abstract thinking itself. Does that make sense?

**Robert:** Yeah, absolutely. Knowing Marvin and knowing his greater body of work — he is playing with elevated diction and the way it addresses elevated ideas, and then undercutting that. Constantly seeking after the musicality of plain speech, the casual remark: *who knows where this came from*, this profound existential moment. That's one of the great things about the range of poetry, and his work in particular — it can encompass the sublime, the absurd, the quotidian, and sweep it all up into this more gestalt experience that is greater than any one of those modes or tones. He was incredibly whimsical, deeply wise, and profoundly unpretentious. That voice comes through, and I think that's a great way of putting it.

**Venkat:** On an orthogonal note — I'm thinking of stylisation and mannerism, and poetry as an abstraction with extra steps almost. One of the differences you see between modern poetry, which feels very much in touch with lived material reality, and medieval poetry in any language: there are very stylised tropes that keep recurring, the same motifs that become almost shibboleths within the community of poets. We were talking about Urdu poetry on the Discord earlier, and that is a genre I've consumed a fair amount of — and it's full of exactly the same recurring motifs: the drinking hall, the cup of wine, the beautiful woman walking in, who you always talk about without reference to specifics. Maybe this is because it's an Islamic art form and you don't want to get too particular. After a while you get bored of mannered and stylised poetry because it stays in this realm. Has there been some evolution there, or a discontinuity in the evolution of poetry away from mannerism and stylisation?

**Robert:** I love the idea of just saying *hey, that's just prose with extra steps.* Because that's a really nice summation of where, for example, William McGonagall makes some very prosaic statements with a few apostrophes and elisions and *O*s here and there and a dash once in a while. Decorating prose — or even decorating familiar motifs — is not poetry per se to me. It's a broad church, but it doesn't strike at the heart of what really makes poetry most effective.

Partly that's because you're looking at an outside-in approach — almost a kind of re-editing of either what's gone before you as tried-and-true motifs that you can use for signalling, or an idea that you had that you want to convey and now you sort of *poetise.* Poetry doesn't work that way. It listens to itself as it goes, and evolves from that.

One of the biggest ways to evoke poetry is through *constraint.* The writing exercises, for example, impose all kinds of constraint that force you to attend and force you to think. These kinds of things show up too in things like improvisational acting, where so many of the rules are just to get you out of your head and get you present — by imposing so much that it almost seems impossible to play the game, and then suddenly you're in a game that transcends those rules as well. There are other art forms that do this, but it's always an inside-out process rather than the other way around. There's a kind of virtue signalling perhaps, but that's usually not what makes for really interesting work. Subverting the virtue signalling makes for interesting work — but again, it has to come through organically, rather than as some imposed or conceptual thing. Poetry knows when you've got an idea in mind. You've got to look at it out of your peripheral vision. You look directly and it just evaporates.

Lots more thoughts — but I'll hand it over to Timber to wrap up, since we're at the hour, and hope to continue the conversation elsewhere.
