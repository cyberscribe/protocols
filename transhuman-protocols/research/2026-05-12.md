- The NYT editors' note records a Pierre Poilievre "quote" that was, in fact, "an A.I.-generated summary of his views about Canadian politics that A.I. rendered as a quotation" — a clean, primary-source instance of fabrication, not paraphrase drift. **Supports A4** at the strongest available register short of formal benchmark: a major-paper correction is institutional admission that the failure mode is real and reaches publication. ## B2 — Protocols and results need to be inspectable

- The reporter "should have checked the accuracy of what the A.I. tool returned" against the source speech (which was publicly available). **Supports B2**: the failure here is precisely an inspectability failure — the AI summary tool's output was treated as ground truth rather than as a claim requiring verification against the underlying corpus. The protocol that should have existed (cross-check AI-quoted speech against the recorded primary source before publication) is a near-trivial extension of standard journalistic verification practice; its absence is the failure. The case also surfaces a subtler inspectability point: when an AI surface produces *formatted* output (quotation marks, attribution), the format itself becomes an implicit truth-claim that bypasses inspection, because humans read formatting as semantically meaningful. Protocols need to address not just whether output is inspected, but whether output formatting can pre-empt inspection. *(case study, primary source)* — [simon-willison-nyt-editors-note-ai-fabricated-quote](read/2026-05-11-simon-willison-nyt-editors-note-ai-fabricated-quote.md)

## Cross-cutting / framework moves

- **Compound failures are the protocol surface.** The Poilievre case is interesting precisely because it is *not* a one-pillar failure. For the false quote to reach print, A4 had to misfire (the model hallucinated quotation-formatted text) *and* B2/B5 had to misfire (no verification, no pre-publication arbitration). Either pillar holding alone would have prevented publication. This is a candidate cross-pillar framework move: protocols don't exist to prevent *AI failures* per se — they exist to prevent the *compound* events where AI failure modes (A1–A4) coincide with human-loop protocol failures (B2–B6). The framework might benefit from an explicit "compound failure" lens — a list of paired (Ax + By) failure modes the protocol must defend against, rather than treating pillar A and pillar B as independent claims.
- **Format-as-implicit-truth-claim as a candidate B2 sub-point.** The detail that the model rendered a summary "as a quotation" is a B2 finding that the current sub-claim phrasing doesn't quite capture. Inspectability is about more than "can the human see what the AI did" — it includes "can the human see what kind of claim the AI's output is making about the world." Quotation marks make a verbatim claim; the same content as paraphrase does not. Worth considering a B2 sub-point: *protocols must defend against AI output whose form implies a stronger epistemic claim than its content warrants*.
