# The Commons Was a Side Effect

*We keep asking whether AI will take our knowledge. The better question is which invisible protocols it quietly switches off.*

I have spent a good part of this year thinking with AI about what thinking with AI might cost us. That is not as much of a paradox as it sounds. It is the whole problem in miniature, and it took me a while to notice.

In an earlier piece I asked what we are delegating to these tools, what we are retaining, and what we are quietly losing the capacity to work out on our own. I want to follow that thread somewhere uncomfortable. Because the thing most at risk is not any particular skill. It is a protocol so old and so quiet that we never gave it a name.

### The fence nobody built

G.K. Chesterton told reformers not to tear down a fence until they knew why it was put there. His rule assumes someone put it there on purpose. The fences I worry about are stranger. Nobody built them. They wove themselves, slowly, out of millions of ordinary acts, until they were holding up fields we never knew were being held.

I think of these as latent protocols. A protocol, in the sense I keep returning to, is just a pattern that coordinates behaviour and then disappears into the background. The handshake. The queue. Peer review. The apprentice learning by standing near the master. The good ones go invisible. We stop seeing the rule and see only the world it quietly makes possible.

Here is one we never named.

### Two things at once

When you work hard at a problem that matters to you, you make two things at once. The first is yours: you get your answer, and in getting it you build the judgment to get the next one. The second slips out into the world almost by accident. The write-up. The reply posted under someone else's question. The fix you mention to a colleague. The shape of a craft that the next person inherits without paying for it.

That second thing is a side effect. Almost nobody struggles through a problem in order to feed the common pool. We do it to solve our own problem, and the pool fills as a byproduct.

Three economists at MIT, among them Daron Acemoglu, gave this a name this year. They call it economies of scope in learning: one effort, producing private skill and a thin public deposit in the same motion. Their conclusion is sobering. The great commons of human knowledge was never anybody's project. It was the exhaust of a protocol nobody wrote down. Effort that learns and contributes at the same time.

And because it is a side effect, nobody is paid for it, nobody defends it, and nobody notices when it stops.

### Ask, and receive

Now hand people a better protocol. Not "struggle, and deposit by accident," but "ask, and receive." A good AI agent is a wonderful answer machine. Used one task at a time, it is often a clean upgrade. Faster, cheaper, frequently sharper. I say that as someone who builds these workflows for clients and watches them earn their keep.

But the new protocol has a missing limb. It hands you the private good and quietly drops the public one. It replaces the struggle, and the struggle was the very thing that happened to fill the well. We do not lose the knowledge first. We retire the protocol first. The well runs dry later.

That is what makes it so easy to miss. Your own work gets better while the ground under everyone thins, because the two effects keep different schedules. Your gain is yours, now. The loss belongs to everyone, later. By the time the well is low, the protocol that refilled it is gone. Worse, the people who might have rebuilt it never learned how, because they grew up asking instead of working things out. This is cognitive debt, run up by one generation and paid by the next.

### The economists' aspirin

So what do the economists prescribe? Make the answer machine worse. Add static, on purpose. Cap how good the AI is allowed to be, so that a little struggle survives.

I understand the logic, and I think it is a mistake. It throws away a real gift for everyone in order to protect a side effect. It needs some referee able to hold the whole world's AI below its ability, which is nobody. And it treats as a fixed knob what is really a design choice. You do not save a hidden protocol by sabotaging its replacement. You save it by working out what it did, and building that back in on purpose.

### A library with no librarians

The obvious fix is to rebuild the commons itself. If AI is fencing off the public store of human knowledge and selling it back to us, then rebuild that store as public infrastructure. Open models, shared data, community-run knowledge pools. This is good and necessary work, and a real movement has formed around it.

But notice what it fixes and what it leaves alone. Rebuilding the commons restores the place the deposit goes. It does nothing about the act of depositing. You can endow the most beautiful library in the world, but if the protocol that wrote the books has been switched off, you have built a mausoleum. It is half a protocol. It answers who owns the well without answering who still draws the water, and how they learn to.

### We have done this before

The real work is older and humbler, and we have already done it once. The calculator put arithmetic on tap two generations ago. We kept drilling arithmetic anyway. Not out of nostalgia, but because someone judged that the struggle built something the answer never could. Number sense. The floor everything later stands on. We looked at a quiet protocol, named the good it carried, and chose to keep it against a better substitute.

That is the move. Do not dull the tool. Redesign the protocol so the answer and the deposit travel together again. Build AI that holds the answer back until you have done the part that teaches you. Build tools where using them puts something back. And ask the one institution whose whole job is keeping worthwhile struggles alive to do exactly that.

That institution is education. It is where we decide which struggles are worth keeping when the shortcut arrives. The catch is order. You can think with AI, but it seems you have to learn to think the slow way first, so there is a self for the partnership to amplify rather than replace. The articulate generalist who can check her own assumptions does not appear by magic. The ladder the next generation needs is the thing most worth protecting, and the thing slipping away fastest.

### The story sets the dial

There is one protocol upstream of all the rest, and it lives in the imagination. We can only build what we can first picture.

The sharpest version of this reached me from an unlikely place. Pope Leo XIV's new encyclical, *Magnifica Humanitas*, refuses both the hype and the dread. It treats AI as a real thing to be met, not a science-fiction monster. And it makes a point worth keeping whatever you believe: even the far-fetched stories, the dreams of perfected post-human beings, matter now. Not because they are about to come true, but because they shape what we let ourselves do today. The story sets the dial.

It frames the whole moment as a choice between two protocols. One it calls Babel: the conceit that a single language, a digital one, can flatten everything, including a person, into data and performance. The other is a shared city, plural and human. And it plants a flag the spreadsheets cannot reach. Human dignity does not depend on what a person produces. I have written before that humanity is not a search space to be optimised. Here is the same conviction in older language. If we let "more efficient" quietly become "more worthy," we will optimise away the people and call it progress.

### Name the fences

So the task is stranger than a policy fight. Before we can decide what to keep, we have to make the invisible visible. Name the protocols that wove themselves while we were not looking. Say out loud what quiet good each one was carrying. Then choose, on purpose, which struggles are load-bearing.

We are very good at building better answer machines. We are not yet in the habit of asking what each one quietly switches off.

The commons was a side effect. So is much of what we mean by knowing: the residue of a species paying attention together, the slow way, and leaving a trail for the ones coming next. The machines will know plenty. The open question is whether we will keep the protocols by which *we* do, and whether we can picture them clearly enough to protect them before they fade.

All of us really is smarter than any one of us. But only if we keep doing the work that makes it true.
