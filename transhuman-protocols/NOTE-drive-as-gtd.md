# The GTD of working with AI

The target is right. It is also a specific commitment, because GTD's shape is not accidental — and
the first thing to be clear about is that **GTD did not win on evidence.** There is no RCT;
Heylighen & Vidal is a theoretical reconstruction (`externalisation-evidence-ledger.md` §3). It won
on five structural things, and DRIVE has one and a half of them.

| what GTD has | DRIVE today |
|---|---|
| a **complete named cycle** — you always know where you are | ✅ Decide · Request · Iterate · Validate · Evolve |
| **one load-bearing noun** — *the trusted system* — that does all the work | ❌ missing |
| **one recurring maintenance act** — the weekly review | ❌ missing |
| an **emotional promise**, not a performance one — *mind like water* | ⚠️ currently sells effectiveness, which is checkable and therefore fragile |
| a **blameless failure diagnosis** — *"your system isn't trusted"* | ❌ missing |
| tool-portable — paper, app, anything | ✅ so far, keep it that way |

The three missing pieces now have candidates, and the point is that **they fall out of the model
rather than being chosen for the brochure.**

## The noun: the working set

GTD's noun is the trusted system. Ours should be **the working set** — the store trimmed to what
one session can actually reference.

It earns the job on four counts: it is concrete and countable; it has a measured optimum behind it
(`Dh ≈ REF_MAX`, `sim/buckets` gate G6, peak clearing both endpoints by ~12× seed noise); it
generates its own maintenance act; and it explains failure without blaming anyone. An unbounded
store is not a virtue and not a moral failing — it is a working set that outgrew its owner.

## The verb: reference it

GTD's power was partly grammatical — *next action*, a physical visible thing. Ours is
**referencing**: going back to what you wrote, for context and orientation.

It is the operation people skip, it is the one that makes externalisation pay at all (writing
without referencing fills the store and empties the head), and it is already our canonical term.

## The maintenance act: ~~the trim~~ — CORRECTED 2026-08-31

**I proposed the trim and it fails this note's own test.** `practices/CLASSIFICATION-2026-08.md`
applied the survivability filter to the three acts this note picked out, and all three failed:

| act | row | why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| the trim | CTX-14 | a **recurring commitment** — it is the weekly review, the exact shape this note warns collapses |
| the check | LOAD-8 | a scheduled check; the only row mapping to `discipline` |
| the rep | DEBT-1 | *keep your hand in* — scheduled, unbound |

That the trim has the best-measured optimum in the bank (G6) made it more attractive, not more
survivable. **Measured and keepable are different properties and I conflated them.**

**The bank already holds in-flow substitutes for two of the three**, and they share one form —
*bound to an event that happens anyway*, rather than to a calendar or to a feeling:

- **the trim → CTX-17, "leave the context better than you found it."** Bound to visiting. Seconds,
  not a commitment.
- **the rep → SOV-10, "answer it yourself first."** Bound to asking.

That is the two-minute rule's grammar, and it is why the two-minute rule survived when the rest of
GTD did not.

### Discipline has no in-flow substitute, and that is the finding

Every `discipline` candidate in 146 rows is a scheduled check. **The parameter the theory is about
has no practice that survives a busy week.** Two readings, and they lead different places:

1. **A missing row.** Something that binds *checking* to an event that occurs anyway, as CTX-17 binds
   trimming and SOV-10 binds the rep. This is a writable design brief, not a lament.
2. **Discipline is not a practice at all — it is a property of practices.** CTX-17 and SOV-10 are
   *already* discipline in flow: both run whether or not you feel the need, because the trigger is
   external to the feeling. On this reading discipline does not take a slot in the shortlist; it is
   **the criterion that makes the other slots survivable**, and "scheduled, not triggered" was
   always a statement about *binding*, not about calendars.

**I would back (2).** It explains why every scheduled candidate fails, it matches the
`cascade.py` vigilance result (a gate whose rate depends on its own recent yield has a hole in it —
and an event-bound gate does not), and it means the product is not "do this ritual" but "bind these
four acts to things you already do." That is teachable in a way a schedule is not.

Test before adopting: check whether the surviving shortlist rows are all event-bound, and whether
the failures are all schedule-bound. If that separation is clean, discipline is the property.

## The promise: staying in control

Not "more effective." Effectiveness is a performance promise, it is checkable, and two of its four
terms have never been measured in the field (`theory-canonical.md` §0's own admission).

**"In control" is already in the definition** — it is what `1/debt` means. It is an emotional
promise of the same kind as *mind like water*, it is what the diagnostic actually measures, and it
is honest, because the thing we can evidence is the denominator.

## The one asset GTD never had

**A diagnostic.** You could never measure whether your GTD system was trusted; you could only
notice it had stopped working. We can measure load and debt, and — if the two-debts split holds
(`NOTE-two-debts.md`) — tell someone *which* debt is theirs and therefore which remedy applies.

That is the commercial engine, and it is a real differentiator rather than a positioning line.

## The warning, and it is the important part

**GTD's full system mostly did not survive contact with people's actual weeks.** What survived is
the two-minute rule, next-actions, and a capture habit. The rest accreted and was abandoned.

So the thing to build is **not a 152-practice system.** It is the two or three acts that survive a
busy week, plus a diagnosis for when they lapse. The practice bank is the *source* to select from,
not the product.

We now have a criterion for selecting — which term of the effectiveness equation a practice moves,
and how much it costs in time — and, new today, a second axis: **which debt it discharges.**
Short-term debt wants indexes and naming and decision records; long-term debt wants deliberate
unaided reps. Those are different products and they should not be sold as one.

## Two limits the classification exposed

**A quarter of the bank leaves nothing behind.** `correctness × neither` is 37 rows — the checking
practices. They move the term the bank cares most about and produce no artefact, so each must be
re-performed from scratch every time. Combined with the rho result (a correlated second gate loses to
one better gate above ρ ≈ 0.26), a large part of the product is *repeated, uncompounding, and often
redundant with the model that produced the work*. That is a criticism of the bank, not of checking.

**The equation cannot see adoption.** Seven rows move no term at all, and they are the governance,
compliance and change-management rows — arguably the most commercially important in the bank. The
effectiveness equation is the right criterion for *which practices work*; it is silent on *whether
anyone does them*, and an enterprise buyer is largely purchasing the second. **Do not let the
equation become the only filter**, or the product optimises for individual efficacy and fails at the
thing that gets it deployed.

**First move on this strand:** classify the registry on both axes, then take the top three by ΔE per
minute and ask whether a person could actually keep doing them. Anything that fails that question is
not a practice, it is a recommendation.
